Over the past several weeks, I facilitated two course discussions—one on water and the other on fire. Preparing these sessions encouraged me to think more carefully about the role of physical elements in my research and about how geospatial methods can remain connected to the environmental processes they seek to represent.

Water and fire are often studied as hazards, resources, or ecological processes. They are also part of lived experience: they shape infrastructure, settlement patterns, public policy, cultural landscapes, and perceptions of place. Thinking through these elements offers me a way to connect environmental processes with the human dimensions of geography.

## Learning from Water

I grew up in Zhejiang, a coastal province in eastern China characterized by a humid climate, extensive waterways, and recurring typhoons. Flooding and infrastructure disruption were familiar features of the regional environment. These experiences gave me an early awareness that water can sustain communities while also creating significant risk.

As the region developed, I observed different approaches to water management, including conventional infrastructure and projects influenced by nature-based design. These changes raised questions that continue to interest me: How should cities adapt to environmental processes that cannot be fully controlled? How can infrastructure reduce risk without separating people from the landscapes and ecosystems around them?

Hangzhou offers a particularly useful example of the relationship between water, culture, and urban development. West Lake is recognized as a cultural landscape shaped through centuries of design, engineering, governance, and artistic interpretation. Its significance does not come from nature or human intervention alone, but from the long interaction between the two.

This perspective has influenced how I understand human–environment relationships. Landscapes are neither purely natural nor entirely constructed. They emerge through continuing interactions among environmental processes, institutions, technologies, and cultural values.

## Water and Risk in California

My studies in California introduced me to a different set of water-related conditions. The state faces both water scarcity and flood risk, often within landscapes shaped by extensive infrastructure and rapid urban development.

During an environmental planning studio, I studied flood risk in Sacramento. The city’s levee system reduces exposure to frequent flooding, but it also raises questions about residual risk: protection can encourage development behind levees while making the remaining possibility of failure less visible. This experience showed me that environmental risk is not determined by physical processes alone. It is also shaped by infrastructure, policy, land-use decisions, and public perception.

I also examined the relationship between wildfire and post-fire debris flows in Montecito. Fire can alter vegetation and soil conditions, changing how water moves through a landscape when heavy rain arrives. Studying these connected processes reinforced the importance of looking beyond individual hazards. Water, fire, vegetation, topography, and settlement patterns operate as part of an interconnected system.

## Understanding Fire as a Process

My interest in fire developed through studying environmental planning and resilience in California. Modern land management has often treated fire primarily as a threat to suppress. However, ecological research and Indigenous land-management practices demonstrate that fire can also play an important role in maintaining ecosystems and reducing the accumulation of combustible vegetation.

This does not make fire harmless. Instead, it shows why environmental processes should be understood in context. The consequences of fire depend on climate, vegetation, land management, development patterns, and social vulnerability. Effective planning therefore requires both physical knowledge and an understanding of how people occupy and manage fire-prone landscapes.

Water and fire also interact. Fire changes vegetation and soil; those changes affect runoff, erosion, and flooding. Treating these elements independently can obscure the processes that produce environmental risk.

## Connecting Environmental Processes with Geospatial Methods

Data-driven geospatial methods offer new ways to observe and model environmental change across large areas and long periods, revealing patterns that would be difficult to analyze manually.

However, predictive performance alone does not guarantee meaningful environmental understanding. A model may detect change without explaining the processes behind it. It may also reproduce limitations in its training data or overlook important differences among places and communities.

For this reason, I am interested in approaches that connect data-driven models with environmental knowledge. In urban microclimate research, for example, vegetation affects temperature through shade, evapotranspiration, and interactions with surrounding buildings and surfaces. Modeling these relationships requires more than identifying statistical patterns; it requires attention to the physical processes that make those patterns meaningful.

Process-aware approaches may help make these methods more interpretable and more relevant to environmental planning. At the same time, human perception and behavior remain essential. People experience heat, flood risk, vegetation, and public space differently depending on their social and geographic context.

## A Direction for Future Research

I want my future research to examine how environmental processes such as water, heat, and fire interact across space, how people perceive and respond to them, and how geospatial methods can represent these relationships more clearly.

This direction brings together several layers of geographic inquiry:

- physical and ecological processes;
- built environments and infrastructure;
- human perception and behavior; and
- methods for observing and modeling change.

For me, the value of these methods lies not only in producing predictions, but also in helping researchers ask better geographic questions. Grounding them in environmental processes and place-based knowledge can make them more interpretable and more useful for planning, climate adaptation, and environmental decision-making.

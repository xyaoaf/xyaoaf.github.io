Over the past few weeks I led two course discussions, one on water and one on fire. Preparing them made me think about how these two elements run through my own research.

I grew up in Zhejiang, a coastal province in eastern China with dense waterways and regular typhoons, so floods were part of ordinary life. Water sustained the region and threatened it at the same time. In Hangzhou, West Lake showed me another side of it: a landscape shaped over centuries by design, engineering, and culture, neither purely natural nor purely built.

California taught me about risk. In a planning studio I studied Sacramento's levees, which prevent frequent floods but encourage building in places that remain exposed if a levee fails. In Montecito I looked at how wildfire changes vegetation and soil, so that the next heavy rain can turn into debris flows. Fire is not only a hazard to suppress, either; ecological research and Indigenous land management show that it can keep ecosystems healthy. What it does depends on climate, vegetation, land management, and where people live.

These experiences shape how I think about geospatial methods. Data-driven models can find patterns across large areas and long periods, but a model that detects change does not necessarily explain it. In my work on urban microclimates, trees cool streets through shade, evapotranspiration, and the way they interact with buildings, and a useful model has to respect those processes. People also experience heat, floods, and greenery differently depending on where and how they live.

In my future research I want to follow how water, heat, and fire interact across places, how people perceive and respond to them, and how geospatial methods can represent these relationships clearly enough to help planning and climate adaptation.
